Detection of Recently Buried Plastic Plates via Disturbed Soil

Information-flow, physical modelling, and sensing feasibility for a 2 m-high drone over plastic plates buried at 15 cm depth.

thermal anomaly • disturbed ground • realistic constraints
BLOCK A Mission Overview & Context
Read first. This block sets scenario and constraints used by all later blocks (B–H).

Scenario

  • Organisers manually bury plastic “mine plates” in a test field, typically a few days before the event.
  • Plates are **plastic**, roughly mine-sized (≈20–25 cm diameter), placed with tops at **15 cm depth**.
  • After burial, soil is backfilled and visually smoothed so the field **looks almost undisturbed**.

Mission Objective

The drone swarm does not need to directly sense plastic; instead it must detect any reliable cues that indicate:

  • “This patch of ground was recently dug and refilled”, and
  • Patch size is consistent with a mine burial, rather than random footprints or small disturbances.

Key Constraints

  • Drone altitude: 2 m above ground during sensing.
  • Budget and weight: hobby-scale micro-UAVs; no heavy military GPR or cooled IR cameras.
  • Burial is **recent** (within days to a few weeks), giving us disturbed-soil signatures to exploit.

High-Level Strategy

Given the depth and material, directly sensing the plate is physically unrealistic from 2 m. Instead we:

  • Model and exploit **disturbed-soil physics**: changes in bulk density, moisture, thermal conductivity, and surface texture.
  • Use a light **thermal IR camera** to detect **surface temperature anomalies** over those disturbed zones.
  • Fuse with RGB texture / shape to confirm patch geometry matches a mine-sized burial.

Concept: the plate at 15 cm depth is effectively invisible; we detect the disturbed soil patch above it using thermal and visual cues.

BLOCK B Soil Physics & Disturbance Effects
Depends on A. For scenario and constraints, see Block A. This block explains what burial does to soil.

What Changes When You Dig and Refill?

Manual burial of a plate in a 30–40 cm wide, ~20 cm deep hole disturbs soil in three main ways:

  1. Bulk density ↓ & porosity ↑ in the disturbed zone (soil is looser, more air-filled).
  2. Moisture distribution changes: small-scale drying or wetting patterns differ from undisturbed soil.
  3. Surface texture & emissivity shift: fine particles accumulate, micro-roughness changes.

Representative Properties

For typical moist agricultural soil, literature values cluster around:

  • Undisturbed layer (0–10 cm): bulk density ρb ≈ 1.5 Mg/m³, thermal conductivity λ ≈ 1.5 W/m·K, volumetric heat capacity Cvol ≈ 2.0 MJ/m³·K.
  • Disturbed layer (0–10 cm above the plate) after hand digging: ρb ≈ 1.2 Mg/m³, λ ≈ 1.1 W/m·K, Cvol ≈ 1.5 MJ/m³·K.
  • LWIR emissivity changes slightly, e.g. from ε ≈ 0.94 (compact) to ε ≈ 0.96 (finer disturbed surface).

These changes mean the disturbed patch:

  • Has lower thermal mass and slower heat conduction downward.
  • Heats and cools faster than undisturbed surroundings.
  • Appears slightly , depending on time of day.
Important: At 15 cm burial depth, the plastic plate’s own thermal influence at the surface is very small. The dominant signal after a few days is the disturbed soil itself, not the object volume.
BLOCK C Simple Thermal Model & Contrast Estimate
Follow after B. Uses soil parameters from Block B to estimate surface temperature contrast.

Energy-Balance Approximation

Consider the top L = 0.10 m soil layer. For a period of strong sunlight (≈2 h) we approximate the temperature rise as:

ΔT ≈ q · Δt / (Cvol · L)

with:

  • Net absorbed flux q ≈ 500 W/m² (sun + sensible heat).
  • Heating duration Δt ≈ 7200 s (2 h).
  • Parameters C_vol from Block B.

Undisturbed vs. Disturbed Regions

  • Undisturbed: Cvol ≈ 2.0 MJ/m³·K ⇒ ΔTund ≈ (500×7200)/(2.0×10⁶×0.1) ≈ 1.8 °C.
  • Disturbed: Cvol ≈ 1.5 MJ/m³·K ⇒ ΔTdist ≈ (500×7200)/(1.5×10⁶×0.1) ≈ 2.4 °C.

So after a couple of hours of sun, the disturbed patch is roughly:

ΔTdist - ΔTund ≈ 0.6 °C warmer

Additional contributors:

  • Lower λ (less downward conduction) steepens the gradient, nudging the peak difference upwards.
  • Slight emissivity difference (ε ≈ 0.96 vs. 0.94) adds ≈0.1–0.3 °C effective radiometric offset.

Combining effects, a realistic **surface temperature contrast** between disturbed patch and background is:

ΔTsurface ≈ 1.0–2.0 °C under favourable clear-sky diurnal cycles.

Temporal Behaviour

The sign of the contrast flips over a diurnal cycle:

  • Morning / early afternoon: disturbed patch warms faster → appears **hotter**.
  • Evening / night: disturbed patch cools faster → appears **cooler**.

Idealised diurnal response: disturbed soil (blue) has larger temperature swings, creating 1–2 °C contrast relative to undisturbed soil (orange).

BLOCK D Thermal Camera & Drone Geometry
Builds on C. Uses ΔT from Block C to predict sensor-level signal at 2 m altitude.

Chosen Sensor Class

We assume a light, UAV-friendly LWIR microbolometer such as:

  • MLX90640 (32×24 pixels, ≈55°×35° FOV, NETD ≈0.1 K at 1 Hz), or
  • FLIR Lepton 3.x (160×120 pixels, ≈57° FOV, NETD ≈0.05–0.07 K).

Ground Sampling Distance at 2 m

For a 55° horizontal FOV camera at 2 m altitude:

  • Ground swath width ≈ 2·h·tan(FOV/2) ≈ 2·2·tan(27.5°) ≈ 2.1 m.
  • With 32 pixels across (MLX90640), each pixel covers ≈ 2.1/32 ≈ 6.5 cm.

A typical disturbed patch from a hand-dug hole (≈35 cm diameter) therefore spans ≈5–6 pixels across, i.e. roughly 25–35 thermal pixels.

Signal vs. Noise

  • From Block C: ΔTsurface ≈ 1–2 °C for disturbed vs undisturbed soil at good times of day.
  • Camera noise (NETD): 0.05–0.1 °C.

Therefore a single pixel fully on the disturbed patch has:

SNR ≈ 10–40 per frame (1–2 °C signal / 0.05–0.1 °C noise).

Even after including some smoothing and mixing at patch edges, cluster-level detection is highly reliable.

From 2 m altitude with ≈55° FOV, a disturbed patch covers multiple thermal pixels, giving good spatial resolution and SNR.

Practical takeaway: under decent weather, a drone-grade LWIR camera at 2 m altitude can clearly resolve recently disturbed patches of mine-like size. The challenge is not raw detectability, but handling clutter (other disturbances, vegetation, weather).
BLOCK E Why Other Sensor Families Fail at 2 m
Optional but important. For design justification, read A then C before this block.

Capacitive / Dielectric Sensing

  • Field lines from a plate electrode at 2 m mostly travel in air; only a tiny fraction penetrates 15 cm into soil.
  • Effective capacitance change from a 20 cm plastic plate under 15 cm soil at 2 m stand-off is ≪10⁻³ of the baseline.
  • Industrial capacitive sensors typically work at millimetre–centimetre distances in air; soil moisture probes are inserted into soil, not flown metres above it.

Conclusion: capacitive sensing from 2 m will see only “bulk soil + air”, with negligible effect from the buried plate or disturbed zone.

Metal Detection / EMI

  • Plastic plates with minimal metal are inherently poor targets for induction-based detectors.
  • Sensitivity of coil-based systems falls roughly as 1/r³. Going from 0.1 m stand-off (handheld detector) to ~2.1 m (drone altitude + burial depth) reduces signal by ~8000×.
  • Mine-clearance detectors are specified for ≤10 cm stand-off and ≤30 cm depth, not metre-scale air gaps.

Conclusion: from a 2 m drone, EMI is effectively blind to plastic plates at 15 cm depth.

Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR)

  • Useful GPR imaging of small plastic mines typically keeps antennas within a few centimetres of the surface.
  • At 1–2 GHz, an extra 2 m air path adds >40 dB free-space loss before soil attenuation and clutter.
  • Contrast between plastic (εr≈3–4) and moist soil (εr≈6–15) is modest; with the power and mass limits of a 500 g drone, SNR at 2 m is not viable.

Acoustic / Seismic Methods

  • Successful acoustic-to-seismic mine detection uses geophones or laser vibrometers near the surface.
  • Pressure fluctuations associated with Rayleigh waves decay extremely quickly with height; microphones at 2 m pick up mostly wind and direct speaker noise.
Design implication: for this competition geometry, thermal + visual disturbance detection is the only physically realistic remote modality. Other sensor families are better suited to ground-contact robots or very low-altitude (<0.2 m) platforms.
BLOCK F Disturbance Cues Beyond Temperature
Use with D. Thermal contrast from Block D is main cue; this block adds complementary visual information.

Micro-topography & Shape

  • Even well-smoothed backfill tends to leave shallow mounds or depressions (5–20 mm amplitude) and altered roughness.
  • With a 12 MP RGB camera at 2 m, ground sampling distance can be ≈1–2 mm per pixel.
  • Shading from a low sun and small changes in texture reveal subtle circular or square patterns consistent with burial holes.
  • Structure-from-motion or stereo (using multiple overlapping images) can reconstruct centimetre-scale height differences for more robust detection, at the cost of extra flight and compute.

Spectral / Moisture Differences

  • Disturbed soil often shows changed reflectance in visible + near-infrared bands due to re-distribution of fine particles and altered moisture.
  • Simple indices (e.g. ratios of NIR to red or green channels) can highlight freshly disturbed patches vs older background, especially on bare soil.
  • However, spectral cues are more sensitive to illumination and surface contamination (dust, plant debris) than thermal cues.

Fusion Strategy

  • Use **thermal** as the primary detector: find hot/cold blobs of the right size at good diurnal times.
  • Confirm with **RGB shape + local texture**: is it roughly round/rectangular and of mine-like dimensions? Is there visible disturbance?
  • Optionally incorporate **simple spectral ratios** to reject patches that look like puddles, shadows, or vegetation.
Goal: we are detecting “recently disturbed patches of mine-like size”, not the plastic plate itself. This is honest, physically realistic, and still operationally useful for safe path planning.
BLOCK G Limitations & Failure Modes
Depends on C–F. This block summarises when the approach works and when it becomes unreliable.

Conditions Where It Works Well

  • Field is mostly **bare soil or sparse low vegetation**.
  • Mines are **buried recently (days–weeks)** and not heavily compacted by machinery afterwards.
  • At least one **clear-sky day** with a normal day–night temperature cycle has occurred after burial.
  • Drone flies during **favourable thermal windows**: shortly after sunrise or in the late afternoon / early evening.

Conditions That Degrade Detection

  • Overcast, windy, or rainy periods smooth out diurnal heating and reduce ΔT below ~0.5 °C.
  • Heavy rain + subsequent drying can recompact the surface and wash fine particles, accelerating decay of the disturbance signature.
  • High clutter environments (vehicle ruts, many footprints, agricultural operations) create multiple disturbed patches of similar size.
  • Dense **vegetation cover** masks soil both thermally and visually.

Fundamental Limits

  • The method cannot reliably detect **old burials** where the soil has fully equilibrated (months–years).
  • It cannot distinguish between “mine burial” and “other large dug hole” if they have similar size and age; classification must rely on context and field rules.
  • Direct detection of **deep or very small objects** remains impossible from 2 m with drone-scale sensors.
Reporting stance: always describe the system as detecting recent disturbed soil patches compatible with mines, and clearly state environmental limitations. This keeps the claims honest while still demonstrating strong practical value within the competition setup.
BLOCK H Design Summary for the Competition
Final block. Assumes you have read Blocks A–G.

Core Claim

Given a drone altitude of 2 m, plastic plates buried at 15 cm, and hobby-scale hardware, the only physically realistic remote sensing strategy is:

  1. Detect **recently disturbed soil patches** via their thermal IR and visual signatures.
  2. Constrain patch size and shape to match probable mine burials.
  3. Use these detections to build a **risk map** and plan a safe corridor for the human.

Key Quantitative Points

  • Disturbed top 0–10 cm has ≈20–30 % lower thermal mass than undisturbed soil.
  • Under clear-sky diurnal heating, this produces **1–2 °C surface temperature contrast** for several days to weeks after burial.
  • Drone-grade LWIR cameras with NETD ≈0.05–0.1 °C at 2 m give **SNR > 10 per pixel** over mine-sized patches.
  • RG B & optional NIR imaging add geometric and spectral cues to reject non-mine disturbances.

Implementation Hooks for Your System Architecture

Within your swarm information-flow (master + scanning drones):

  • Implement a **thermal anomaly detector** on each scanning drone:
    • Inputs: temperature grid from LWIR camera, daylight/timing info.
    • Outputs: “disturbance candidates” with location, size, ΔT, and confidence.
  • Fuse candidates into a **global disturbance map** on the master drone, alongside any prior map information.
  • Inflate disturbance regions by a safety buffer and use them as **obstacles** in the path-planning layer.
  • Communicate the resulting **safe corridor** to the human via formation and LED signalling, as in your existing design.

How to Present This in the Report

In your write-up, you can summarise it roughly as:

Our platform is not attempting to “see” the plastic plate itself from 2 m altitude; physics and sensor limits make that unrealistic at 15 cm burial depth. Instead, we deliberately target the disturbed soil created when organisers dig, place, and backfill the plate. This disturbed zone has measurably different bulk density, moisture, and thermal properties, which yield a 1–2 °C surface temperature anomaly for several days under normal diurnal cycles. A lightweight LWIR camera on each drone, combined with RGB imagery, detects and localises these anomalies with high SNR, letting the swarm mark mine-sized disturbed patches and plan safe, low-risk routes around them.
